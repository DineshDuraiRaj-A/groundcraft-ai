Coral Reefs: An Overview

Coral reefs are underwater ecosystems built by colonies of tiny animals called coral polyps. Each polyp secretes a hard calcium carbonate skeleton, and over centuries these skeletons accumulate into the large reef structures visible from space, such as Australia's Great Barrier Reef.

Formation and Growth
Reefs typically grow in warm, shallow, clear water, usually within 30 degrees of the equator. Coral polyps have a symbiotic relationship with microscopic algae called zooxanthellae, which live inside the coral's tissue. The algae photosynthesize and share nutrients with the coral, while the coral provides the algae with a protected environment and compounds needed for photosynthesis. This relationship is why most reef-building coral needs sunlight and cannot survive in deep or murky water.

Biodiversity
Although reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor, they support approximately 25% of all known marine species at some point in their life cycle, including fish, mollusks, crustaceans, and sea turtles. This density of life has led scientists to describe reefs as the "rainforests of the sea."

Threats
Rising ocean temperatures cause coral bleaching, a stress response in which coral expels its zooxanthellae, losing both its color and its main energy source. Prolonged bleaching events can lead to coral death if temperatures do not return to normal quickly enough. Ocean acidification, caused by increased carbon dioxide absorption, also makes it harder for coral to build its calcium carbonate skeleton. Additional threats include overfishing, coastal pollution, and physical damage from anchors and careless tourism.

Conservation Efforts
Marine protected areas, coral nurseries, and selective breeding of heat-resistant coral strains are among the strategies currently used to slow reef decline. Some researchers are also experimenting with assisted evolution, exposing coral to gradually increasing temperatures to encourage natural heat tolerance across generations.
